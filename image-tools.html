Guide

Getting image requirements right the first time

Almost every college application, exam registration, and job portal asks for a photo or a document image in a very specific format, and rejects the file quietly or with a vague error if you miss the mark. Most of these rejections come down to one of three things: the wrong file type, a file that is too large or too small, or dimensions that do not match what the form expects. None of this reflects on you, it is simply a technical mismatch, and it is usually fixed in under a minute once you know which tool to reach for.

Match the format before you match anything else

JPG remains the most widely accepted format for photographs because it compresses well without needing transparency. PNG is the better choice for anything with sharp edges or text, such as a scanned signature or a screenshot, since it does not introduce the soft blur that JPG compression can leave around fine lines. WebP tends to give the smallest file at a given quality level, but not every government or institutional portal accepts it yet, so it is worth checking the accepted format list on a form before you rely on it.

File size limits exist because of the systems on the other end

A limit of 100 KB or 200 KB for a photograph is not arbitrary. Many application portals store thousands of these files and were built years ago on infrastructure that assumes small uploads. Reducing quality by even a small amount usually cuts file size dramatically, because compression removes the fine detail human eyes barely notice while keeping the parts that make an image recognizable. If a form gives both a minimum and a maximum size, aim for the middle of that range rather than the lowest number, since some portals reject files that are unusually small as well as ones that are too large.

Exact pixel dimensions matter for signatures and passport photos specifically

Unlike a general photo upload, signature and passport photo fields are often validated against an exact width and height in pixels or millimeters, and a file that is even slightly off can be rejected automatically before a human ever reviews it. This is why the passport photo and signature tools on this page work to precise output dimensions rather than just scaling an image down proportionally, and why the passport tool lets you select a recognized specification rather than guessing at one.

A short checklist before you submit

  • Re-read the exact requirement on the form, including whether it is written in pixels, millimeters, or inches, since these are not interchangeable without a DPI setting.
  • Check both the minimum and maximum file size if both are listed, not just the maximum.
  • Open the final file once after downloading it, to confirm it looks correct and is not accidentally rotated or cropped oddly.
  • Keep a copy of the original, unedited photo somewhere safe, since you will likely need to prepare the same photo differently for a second form later.

None of these tools store or transmit your images. Every conversion, crop, and resize happens directly in your browser using your device's own processing, and the file never leaves your computer until you choose to download it.
